[[slnc 800]] The full processing chain. [[slnc 600]]

Start with a known waveform. The channel delays, weakens, and alters the returning echo. A matched filter turns delay into a sharp range feature. Across repeated pulses, phase change becomes Doppler velocity. A tracker can smooth noisy measurements over time. [[slnc 350]]

With several antenna elements, relative phase across the array adds direction. A direction scan can identify where energy arrives. Steering weights then point the array toward the target, while a constrained null can reject an interferer from another angle. [[slnc 350]]

The processing order matters because each result uses a different physical signature: delay for range, pulse-to-pulse phase for velocity, and element-to-element phase for direction.

[[slnc 800]] First artifact: the chirp. [[slnc 600]]

The waveform panel shows the real part of a linear frequency-modulated chirp over 20 microseconds. Its instantaneous oscillation rate changes across the pulse. That frequency sweep is what the matched filter later recognizes. [[slnc 350]]

The chirp contains 400 samples at the chosen 20-megahertz sampling rate. With five megahertz of bandwidth, the ideal range-resolution scale is about 30 metres. The waveform is the deliberate signal we send, so every later measurement can compare an echo with something known.

[[slnc 800]] Second artifact: a range peak. [[slnc 600]]

Place a copy of the chirp after a two-way travel delay corresponding to a target near one thousand metres. The matched filter slides the known chirp across the received signal and measures where they align. [[slnc 350]]

Instead of a long echo waveform, we get a compact peak at the target's delay. Converting that delay to distance gives the range estimate. Sampling can place the peak on a nearby discrete range bin, so read the result as approximately one thousand metres, not as an infinitely precise distance. In the notebook, the matched-filter peak reads back as about 997 metres against a true value of 1,000 metres. [[slnc 350]]

This is the range result introduced earlier in the track. It becomes one axis of the range-Doppler map.

[[slnc 800]] Third artifact: velocity across pulses. [[slnc 600]]

Repeat the echo over 64 pulses while the target moves at 20 metres per second. The target's phase advances from pulse to pulse. A slow-time Fourier transform measures the rate of that phase change. [[slnc 350]]

The range-Doppler map now places energy near one thousand metres and positive 20 metres per second. Range came from delay within each pulse. Velocity came from phase evolution across pulses. The two coordinates describe one moving return in this demonstration; in the notebook, the bin at 1,000 metres and 20 metres per second stands out clearly from the surrounding scene. [[slnc 350]]

The tracker's job is different again: it would combine repeated noisy detections into a smoother estimate over time. That stage is part of the full chain even though it is not one of the final figure's four panels.

[[slnc 800]] Fourth artifact: the array adds angle. [[slnc 600]]

Eight antennas at half-wavelength spacing form the spatial instrument. Their beam pattern has a main lobe, nulls, and sidelobes. A wave from plus 20 degrees produces a specific progression of phase across those eight elements. [[slnc 350]]

The notebook constructs a covariance estimate for a plus-20-degree source and runs a Bartlett direction scan. The spatial spectrum peaks near that angle. This angle calculation is a separate demonstration using the same array geometry, not an angle read directly from the earlier range-Doppler data. [[slnc 350]]

In a full radar, the range-Doppler detection and array snapshot would be connected so that all coordinates refer to the same return.

[[slnc 800]] Fifth artifact: steer and place a null. [[slnc 600]]

Steering weights make the array respond fully to the plus-20-degree target. But the steered beam still has sidelobes. The earlier lesson showed why a strong source at minus 30 degrees can leak through. [[slnc 350]]

The constrained weights keep unit response at plus 20 degrees and force zero response at minus 30 degrees in the ideal array model. The beam-pattern overlay shows both curves. Target gain stays at zero decibels relative to unity, while the interferer direction becomes a deep null. In the notebook, the steering-only curve sits about minus 18.6 decibels at the interferer, while the constrained curve drops to about minus 319 decibels, effectively a numerical zero. [[slnc 350]]

That overlay shows the spatial design. It does not claim that this notebook has rerun the interfered range-Doppler scene from notebook 09.

[[slnc 800]] Two integration mistakes to avoid. [[slnc 600]]

First, do not treat four panels with common labels as proof that one data cube flowed through every algorithm. Here the calculations are linked by baseline choices, but some panels are separate simulations. A true end-to-end test would carry the same received array data through range, Doppler, angle, and nulling. [[slnc 350]]

Second, do not choose a null angle without a source for that direction. A direction scan, or another angle estimate, must tell the array where the interferer is. The null works because its constraint names a physical arrival direction.
